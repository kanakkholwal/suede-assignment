# Customer interview packet

## About this packet

The five conversations below are fictional, anonymized composites created for the Catalog Lab Interview. They are qualitative source material, not a record of real interviews, a statistically representative study, or a feature specification.

The names, companies, quantities, and quotes are invented.

---

## 1. The constrained family-room sofa

**Participant:** D-01, independent residential designer in a three-person studio

**Context:** D-01 is sourcing a family-room sofa for a client with two young children, a medium-sized dog, and a narrow townhouse living room. The client has sent several aspirational images but wants the room to stay calm rather than precious.

**What happened in the last project**

> “I started with ‘sofa’ because that is honestly how I think at first. Then I had 60 things that were technically sofas, and I began opening tabs one by one. I needed something around 84 to 96 inches, not white, not a huge cloud shape, reasonably deep, and something that would arrive before the room install. The important words were in the product pages, if they were there at all, not in the search result.”

> “The client kept asking, ‘Can you give me three that all feel like this but at different investment levels?’ I could make that list, but I made it by remembering brands and searching the web. The catalog gave me things I already knew how to find.”

D-01 described several non-negotiables: a seat deep enough for lounging, a fabric that could plausibly handle children, a maximum outside width, and a delivery date. They also cared about silhouette, but said it was easier to hold that as a judgment than to translate into a filter. A “bouclé” tag alone was not useful: some pieces looked too precious; some were simply wrong at the room scale.

**How D-01 searches today**

> “I type a sentence even though I know systems rarely understand it: ‘warm, durable family room sofa under six thousand, 90 inches, in stock.’ It is my way of explaining the brief. If nothing happens, I go back to a spreadsheet. I do not want to become a database administrator before I can start designing.”

> “I use brand sites when I need the truth. I use saved folders when I need memory. I would use a central catalog when it helps me make a recommendation faster—not when it sends me to a different place to do the real work.”

When asked what a useful result would look like, D-01 did not ask for a single answer. They wanted a short starting set and a way to understand why each was included: “This is closer to the budget; this is more durable; this is your fast ship option; this is the form-forward one.” They would rather see uncertainty disclosed than have an apparently exact delivery answer that later changes.

**A moment of frustration**

> “A search result that says ‘sofa’ is not a result. It is a category page with extra steps. The useful question is: what can I responsibly show this client on Thursday?”

## 2. The value comparison

**Participant:** D-02, senior designer at a hospitality and residential practice

**Context:** D-02 is presenting options for a lobby lounge. The principal likes the visual language of an expensive sculptural chair, while the client has asked for a tiered proposal. The project has a budget conversation in two days.

**What they are trying to accomplish**

> “The question is almost never ‘find me a chair.’ It is ‘give me the version that makes the room feel intentional at $700, $1,500, and $4,000.’ If I show three random chairs at three prices, it looks like I have not done the thinking.”

> “Comparable means something more than same category. It can be the same curve, same visual weight, same kind of comfort, the same contract-grade concern, or the same ability to carry the room. The system cannot know all of that from a title.”

D-02 currently constructs these tiers from accumulated brand knowledge, image searches, vendor reps, and previous presentations. They described the catalog as potentially useful for surfacing a “first pass,” but not a substitute for their judgment. A comparison is successful when they can confidently narrate the trade: “This one buys the silhouette; this one buys durability; this one gets us the finish date.”

**What is difficult now**

> “Price is oddly hard. Sometimes it is an entry price for the smallest configuration. Sometimes it is one finish. Sometimes it is stale. I would rather the product say ‘starting at, source captured on this date’ than show a false certainty.”

> “If you are going to recommend alternatives, do not give me five things that are all from the same brand or all technically in budget but visually unrelated. I need range without noise.”

They also want to compare a product they are already looking at, rather than begin a completely new search. In their ideal flow, they would open an item and ask for “similar but less expensive,” then tune the reference point: similar in material, in form, in dimensions, or in use case.

**The tricky part**

D-02 was wary of opaque recommendations:

> “I am perfectly happy to use a smart tool. I just need it to behave like a good junior designer: show me the evidence, let me reject bad directions quickly, and do not act certain about things it cannot know.”

They might accept an early version that is “search plus saved preferences plus a clear explanation,” even if it is not a fully automated recommendation engine. They specifically said that a good comparison tool could make them discover lesser-known brands—but only if the tool did not flatten every distinctive object into generic “mid-century modern.”

## 3. The commercially confident studio

**Participant:** D-03, founder of a growing online-first interior studio

**Context:** D-03 manages the sourcing pipeline and is training a junior designer. The studio has a mix of direct retail links, trade-program products, and made-to-order pieces. They are trying to improve both project economics and the consistency of recommendations.

**How the studio thinks about brands**

> “When a junior asks, ‘What brand should I start with?’ I do not have one answer. It depends on whether we are furnishing a rental, a warm family room, a small dining nook, or a higher-budget primary suite. Price point and room type are the shortcut to the right mental shelf.”

> “There are brands I would reach for when we need polish under a certain number, brands for a specific sculptural moment, and brands I know are operationally painful. That knowledge lives in people’s heads.”

D-03 wants an assistant that can make grounded brand suggestions such as “for a $2–4k living-room seating budget, here are three brands you commonly use and why.” But they do not want a black-box brand ranking. They want to teach the system studio preferences over time and understand when the recommendation comes from a price band, a room fit, previous use, or a data gap.

**Commercial information**

> “I do care about what we make money on. Not because I want to push the worst option—we get repeat business by being good—but because I cannot run a studio on vibes. If two options serve the client equally well, I need to know which one supports the work.”

> “That is sensitive. I would not put a giant margin badge on a client-facing screen. But internally I might want to prioritize products where the economics are known, versus products where we have no idea.”

The studio currently keeps markup, trade discounts, freight exposure, and return risk in separate documents. D-03 said the largest issue is not the calculation itself; it is deciding which inputs can be trusted. “If the catalog only has a modeled margin, say it is modeled. If freight is unknown, that could change the answer more than a two-point margin difference.”

**What they would do with better support**

> “I want the junior to have a good starting place, not permission to stop thinking. They should be able to say: we chose this because it fits the room, it is within the client’s investment level, and it has a known path to purchase. Then they can make an aesthetic judgment.”

They would value an internal workflow that calls attention to an economic tradeoff only when it is relevant. For example, the studio may choose a lower-margin chair because it completes the room or because a client strongly prefers it. Conversely, if a large proposal has several equivalent options, the studio may want a quiet signal that a more reliable commercial path exists.

## 4. The distinctive-source seeker

**Participant:** D-04, owner of a small high-touch residential practice

**Context:** D-04 is known for mixing recognizable foundational pieces with niche lighting, vintage-adjacent objects, and smaller makers. They feel mainstream catalog results can make every project converge.

**What “niche” means to them**

> “I do not mean obscure for the sake of obscure. I mean something with a point of view. Maybe it is a maker who does an unusual paper lamp; maybe it is a small-batch piece that has texture without looking crafty; maybe it is simply a brand I have not already seen on ten Instagram boards.”

> “If I search ‘pendant,’ I will get the things the system knows are pendants. That does not help me discover a beautiful lantern-like light for an entry.”

D-04 wants discovery to be connected to a brief, but resists over-specifying at the start. They want to browse “in a direction” and have the catalog offer paths that are less obvious. They cited room mood, material, cultural references, craft, scale, and visual texture as real criteria—while acknowledging that some are hard to structure.

**A concern about recommendation systems**

> “The danger is a system that thinks distinctive means one tag. Then every query for ‘interesting’ becomes the same boucle chair and mushroom lamp. That is not taste; it is a stereotype.”

> “Give me enough context to understand why something appeared. Is it a material match? A newer maker? A visual counterpoint? Let me say ‘more like this, but quieter’ or ‘less obvious, but still feasible.’”

D-04 also described a pragmatic constraint: the more niche an item, the more likely they need help validating source quality, returns, shipping, and timing. They do not expect every maker to have complete fields in a catalog. They do expect a distinction between “we know this” and “we have not checked this.”

**How they evaluate a good result**

> “A good discovery tool gives me one thing I had not considered and makes me want to investigate it. A bad one gives me fifteen things that feel machine-generated and asks me to call that inspiration.”

They might prefer a small, editorially diverse result set over an infinite grid. At the same time, they said they would not want a tool to hide familiar brands entirely: a client needs anchors. The more useful question may be how a niche option relates to a known option, not whether one replaces the other.

## 5. The operationally cautious presenter

**Participant:** D-05, project manager and designer at a mid-sized residential studio

**Context:** D-05 is assembling a client presentation for a home that needs to be installed before a move-in. They have been burned by stock claims, vendor changes, and client-facing links that later turn out not to be viable.

**The job behind “in stock only”**

> “When I say in stock, I do not actually mean a green badge. I mean: can I make a presentation promise without creating a fire drill for someone else?”

> “Some vendors call it in stock when it is in a warehouse but needs a freight quote. Some have one color. Some will say available and then the order confirmation says twelve weeks. I need a system to tell me what it knows, when it knew it, and what still needs a human check.”

D-05 would use a simple “in stock only” filter as an early cut, especially for an urgent job, but said it is insufficient by itself. They need stock state, lead time, and confidence to be legible enough to make a judgment. An unknown status is not a negative result; it is a reason to assign a follow-up.

**How they prepare a final list**

> “The final pass before we send is an exception hunt. Which items have an uncertain lead time? Which are over budget after freight? Which one has no source link? Which choices are no longer coherent together? The catalog could help most if it lets me see what is missing—not just what is there.”

D-05 is open to an AI-assisted research step that summarizes known evidence or drafts questions for a vendor. They would not let an AI-generated answer substitute for a time-sensitive confirmation. In their words: “Use the machine to help us find the holes quickly. Do not let it fill the holes with confidence.”

> “Reviews can be a useful signal, but I need to know the source, date, and project context. A glowing review does not tell me that the item is still in stock or appropriate for this installation.”
